ASTRØ-NØVA autonomous rover

A small-scale exploration rover inspired by planetary missions. It drives itself from A to B with A* route planning, avoids obstacles, takes photos on demand with its camera payload and sends telemetry and images to a ground station over LoRa. Designed, built and tested by a team of three; every result on this page was measured on the built rover.

The mission

Planetary rovers like Curiosity and Perseverance can't be driven in real time from Earth. The communication delay is too long, so they have to follow planned routes and avoid obstacles on their own while they explore and send back data. ASTRØ-NØVA recreates that mission at small scale.

On a flat, mapped area with fixed limits, the rover had to reach a target point without anyone driving it, avoiding real obstacles detected by its sensors and virtual ones defined in software. Along the way, its camera payload had to take inspection photos on demand, and the ground segment had to receive telemetry and images in real time. Everything had to fit within a 20 × 20 cm footprint.

What I did

  • Designed the rover's entire mechanical CAD: a modular two-level chassis on a 20 × 20 cm footprint, with the battery bay, motor mounts, sensor walls, camera servo mount and cover, 3D printed in PLA with TPU tires.
  • Programmed the ground control interface in Python: the grid map with physical and virtual obstacles, manual and autonomous modes, live telemetry, current and signal plots with CSV export, and photo capture.
  • Programmed part of the autonomous motion, mainly the stepper-motor step calculation for the rover's 90° turns, and some of the rover's sensors.
  • Soldered components and ran most of the tests on the assembled rover: moves, turns and error cases.
  • Analyzed the rover's power consumption in MATLAB from the current data logged through the interface.

Systems engineering approach

The project followed a systems engineering approach, run like a small space mission: from the mission objectives to a functional architecture for each subsystem, defined interfaces, component-level tests, simulation, integration and full-system validation.

Key design decisions

The main choices and the reason behind each one.
DecisionWhy
Two-level modular chassisKeeps the motors and batteries low in the base and the electronics on top, for a stable chassis that is easy to assemble and maintain.
PLA structure, TPU tires and a caster wheelPLA for the printed structure, TPU for traction, and a metal caster as a third support point for smooth, stable motion.
Removable front coverQuick access to the batteries for replacement and inspection.
Stepper motors with microsteppingMoves counted in steps are precise and repeatable, so the rover can advance exactly one grid cell or turn exactly 90°; microstepping reduces positioning error.
A* route planning on a gridFinds short routes without exploring every option and behaves predictably, which suits a rover that moves in ordered, discrete steps.
A* planning on the ground segmentThe map, the destination and the virtual obstacles live in the ground control interface, the logic was first built and tested on a computer, and the operator can watch the route and change the destination or obstacles during the mission. As in many planetary rover drives, the route is planned on the ground and executed by the rover.
A* tested in Processing firstThe navigation logic could be debugged without depending on the rover being available.
LoRa radio linkLong range and low power for commands, telemetry and images.
Separate camera board (ESP32-CAM)The camera board only takes and sends photos, while the rover's controller handles everything else.
Photos sent in fragments with retriesThe ground station requests each fragment and asks again if one is lost, so a dropped packet doesn't ruin the photo.
I2C multiplexer for the distance sensorsThe three sensors share the same address, and changing it caused conflicts on the bus.
Custom PCBsOrdered, reliable connections; the team credits the PCBs with reducing errors.
Non-blocking motor control, a watchdog and four threads in the interfaceThe motors move without freezing the rover's program, the watchdog prevents lockups, and the interface stays responsive while the camera, keyboard and buttons run in parallel.
MPU6050 not used to correct orientationIt lost precision in slow turns, and the rover doesn't turn fast enough to benefit from it.

Mechanical design

The chassis has two levels. The base holds the 4 × 18650 batteries, the NEMA 17 motors, the caster wheel, the switch and a removable front cover. The control level holds the PCB, a 2.5 cm wall for the distance sensors and the mount for the servo and the camera. The rover is 13 cm tall, or 17.5 cm with the camera, with hexagonal openings in the cover. It is printed in PLA, with TPU on the tires and M3 screws.

Electronics and power

The team designed three custom PCBs: one for the rover, one for the camera and one for the ground station. The battery feeds the motor drivers directly and, through a 5 V regulator, the ESP32, the camera and the servo. An INA219 monitors current, voltage and power.

Navigation

The rover moves by counting steps of its stepper motors, at 6,400 steps per turn: one 33.7 cm cell takes 10,644 steps and a 90° turn takes 4,912 steps per motor. The values come from the calculation below and were then adjusted by testing on the real floor.

Step calculation for one cell and for a 90° turn, with 6,400 steps per wheel turn.
CalculationLinear advance (one cell)Turn of 90°
Wheel circumference, π × 6.45 cm20.26 cm20.26 cm
Distance the wheel must travel33.7 cm15.55 cm, a quarter of the turning circle (π × 19.8 cm = 62.203 cm)
Wheel turns needed33.7 / 20.26 = 1.663115.55 / 20.26 = 0.7675
Steps, turns × 6,40010,6444,912 per motor

The team used A*, with f(n) = g(n) + h(n), calculated in the ground control interface and first tested in a Processing simulation on a 20 × 20 grid with virtual sensors.

In autonomous mode, the rover follows the route cell by cell. If it detects an obstacle, the route is recalculated, and if it ends up surrounded, it backs up. A FIFO list of commands lets the system revert the last move after a communication error.

Manual mode respects the map limits and the obstacles.

Camera payload

The payload is an ESP32-CAM on a servo that points it left, front or right. On command, it takes a photo with or without flash at about 320 × 240 and passes it to the rover over UART. The rover sends it to the ground station in fragments over LoRa; the ground station requests each fragment and retries up to 10 times, every 2 seconds, until it arrives. The interface shows the progress, displays the photo and saves it for later analysis.

Ground control interface

  • A map with physical and virtual obstacles.
  • Manual and autonomous modes.
  • Camera control.
  • Current and RSSI plots.
  • Telemetry every second.
  • CSV export and import.
  • Four threads, so the interface doesn't block.

Limitations and next steps

Limitations

  • Position comes only from counting motor steps, with no encoder feedback.
  • The MPU6050 lost precision in slow turns, so it was not used to correct orientation.
  • The distance sensors struggled with very dark black surfaces.
  • The rover discovers obstacles only one cell ahead.
  • Autonomous navigation depends on the LoRa link: each move is sent from the ground, so if the link drops, the rover stops and waits.

Next steps

  • Add wheel encoders and an orientation controller with the MPU6050.
  • Use triangulation to locate the rover.
  • Detect obstacles more than one cell ahead.
  • Use the camera to identify the type of obstacle.
  • Add a mechanism to collect samples.
  • Move route planning on board, so the rover can keep navigating if the link drops.

What I learned

Mechanical design is integration design. Drawing the chassis meant fitting four batteries, two motors, three distance sensors, a camera on a servo and the PCB into 20 × 20 cm, while leaving room for wiring, screws and quick battery access. Every part I added in CAD had to work for the electronics and for whoever had to assemble and fix it.

Testing is where the system really comes together. The step counts calculated on paper had to be adjusted through testing on the real floor, because without encoders the rover only knows where it is by counting steps. Running moves, turns and error cases again and again is what made the navigation repeatable.

Measure before you conclude. The estimate said the battery would last 2 h 39 min. Logging the real current through the interface and analyzing it in MATLAB showed 4 h 28 min, because the heaviest loads only run at specific moments.
