Automated robotic box folding cell

A robotic cell that folds carton boxes in four sizes, replacing a manual task that scored 6 on the RULA ergonomic scale. It was designed with Carrier and sized with a digital twin. Every result on this page is a projection from the simulation model.

The challenge

Four operators, two per shift on two shifts, folded carton boxes by hand at 11 seconds per box, for 1,309 boxes a day across four box sizes. The task is repetitive, forces awkward postures and takes physical effort. The project was to design a robotic cell that handles all four sizes and meets the ANSI/RIA R15.06-1999 safety standard, and to check in simulation what it would deliver.

What I did

  • Designed the cell layout integrating two UR10 collaborative robots, vacuum grippers, linear rails, conveyors and PLC-based control, with a Cognex vision system that detects lid size and automatically adjusts the fixture across four box formats.
  • Modeled material flow, cycle times, bottlenecks and work in process in Siemens Tecnomatix Plant Simulation, and validated robot trajectories in Process Simulate to size cell capacity.
  • Projected an increase in daily output from 1,309 to 1,440 boxes and a 75% reduction in required headcount, from 4 operators to 1, with a payback period of 1.8 years.
  • Worked in a team of four that also covered the ergonomic analysis, safety design, budget and financial analysis.

Ergonomic analysis

The case for automating started with the people doing the job. The team scored the manual task with RULA, which rates the posture of the neck, trunk and upper limbs. The result was 6 out of 7, action level 3: changes needed soon, through redesign, task rotation or automation.

The Job Strain Index (JSI) multiplies six factors: intensity of exertion, duration of exertion, efforts per minute, hand and wrist posture, speed of work and hours per day. For the manual process it came out at 108.

Cell design

Two UR10 robots, each rated for 10 kg with a 1,300 mm reach and ±0.1 mm repeatability, ride on linear rails around a central folding fixture, so both can work on the same box in parallel. A Cognex camera reads each lid's size and checks it for defects, and a PLC coordinates the robots, grippers, sensors and conveyors.

  • Custom vacuum grippers with 16 TXN-38 suction cups across two grippers, holding with 288 N each.
  • An adjustable fixture with 13 vertical plates that guide and hold the lid and four raised plates that fold the inner flaps. It resizes itself on its own linear rail.
  • Modular conveyors that carry the lids between inspection, folding and output.

How the cell works

  1. Load and inspect. The carton lid goes on the input conveyor. A Cognex camera detects its size and checks it for defects such as stains or deformation.
  2. Adjust the fixture. A presence sensor stops the conveyor and tells the PLC. The fixture moves on its own rail to the detected size.
  3. Pick. A UR10 on its linear rail moves to the box and picks it with the suction gripper.
  4. Place. The rail moves the robot in front of the fixture, and the robot places the box on it.
  5. Fold. Both robots push the box down to trigger the fixture, which folds the main flaps to 90°. Then one robot holds a side flap while the other folds the adjacent one, and finally each robot folds one of the last two flaps at the same time.
  6. Hand off. The second robot picks up the folded box and places it on the output conveyor.

Safety

The cell was designed against ANSI/RIA R15.06-1999. It has perimeter fencing that keeps people out of the robot area, doors with sensors, emergency stops, signal towers that show normal, warning or emergency state, and signs for hazard zones and required protective equipment. Presence sensors at the box inputs and outputs, not modeled in CAD, report to the PLC to keep the sequence in order.

Results

The team built a digital twin in two tools: Process Simulate for the cell itself, with the robots, rails, conveyors, grippers and fixture running the full sequence, and Plant Simulation to compare the manual process against the cell. Output rises 10.01% and the headcount falls 75%.

What I learned

Start from the people doing the work. I learned how to take an automation from scratch, beginning with the problem and its ergonomic risk. RULA and JSI turned "this job is hard on the body" into numbers, and showed me that a solution has to be safe and comfortable for workers, not only efficient.

Simulate before you build. Process Simulate and Plant Simulation let us validate the whole design before building anything. They showed that the cell raises output, cuts the headcount and, above all, lowers the physical strain on operators.

Decisions need evidence. Every choice had to rest on a safety standard such as ANSI/RIA, on system performance or on the return on investment.

Details decide the design. From choosing the robot to placing sensors and actuators and planning motion paths, small details shaped the result. I also saw how continuous improvement and Lean thinking apply inside a robotic cell.
